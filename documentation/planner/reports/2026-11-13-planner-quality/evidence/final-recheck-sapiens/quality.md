- Syllabus **Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind — Yuval Noah Harari** from model

Topics:

1. **An Animal of No Significance** w2 d1: Trace the timeline from early hominins to Homo sapiens; Explain the Cognitive, Agricultural and Scientific Revolutions as the book's frame; Account for the belief that sapiens displaced other human species; Weigh the debate over the book's sweeping claims and its critics
2. **The Tree of Knowledge** w2 d2: Explain why the Cognitive Revolution let sapiens imagine shared fictions; Distinguish the limits of chimpanzee and Neanderthal communication; Describe fictive language as the basis of large-scale cooperation; Illustrate how myths enable trade, religion and states
3. **A Day in the Life of Adam and Eve** w2 d2: Reconstruct forager diet, work hours, health and social life; Contrast the 'original affluent society' view with evidence of hardship; Evaluate foraging life against later farming and urban living; Note the tension between Harari's claims and archaeological evidence
4. **The Flood** w2 d2: Explain the role of fictive language in the Cognitive Revolution; Trace how sapiens spread across continents and replaced other humans; Assess the evidence for the extinction of megafauna and other species; Explain why agriculture was not an inevitable step
5. **History's Biggest Fraud** w2 d2: Describe the shift from foraging to farming and its timeline; Explain the luxuries-become-necessities trap; Assess costs of farming: diet, disease, workload and violence; Explain why domestication was driven by plants and wheat, not people
6. **Building Pyramids** w2 d2: Explain imagined orders and how they embed in material life; Describe the role of writing and bureaucracy in large societies; Show how hierarchies of gender, class and race were justified; Evaluate how orders shape desires and appear natural
7. **Memory Overload** w1 d2: Describe the invention of writing and its role in record-keeping; Explain how writing moved from data to ideas; Explain why writing was needed for large-scale states; Assess how writing and bureaucracy created and preserved hierarchies
8. **There is No Justice in History** w1 d3: Explain how imagined hierarchies underpin gender, race and class; Describe the 'myth of purity' and cultural bias; Explain how unifications produce both order and injustice; Critically assess evidence on historical inequality
9. **The Arrow of History** w1 d2: Explain the fusion of global cultures via empires, trade and religion; Describe how money created universal exchange and trust; Analyse empire as a mechanism of homogenisation; Explain the role of religion in creating universal order
10. **The Scent of Money** w1 d1: Explain how money works as shared trust; Distinguish barter, gift exchange and monetary systems; Explain the role of coins, credit and minting in markets; Describe the moral ambivalence attached to money
11. **Imperial Visions** w1 d2: Explain empire as an agent of cultural and political unification; Explain the 'imperial cycle' in history; Trace the line from ancient empires to modern global culture; Assess empire's inclusive and destructive sides
12. **The Law of Religion** w2 d3: Distinguish animism, polytheism, monotheism and dualism; Explain humanist, natural-law and other non-theistic religions; Show how religion unifies large groups; Examine the role of religion in the Scientific Revolution
13. **The Secret of Success** w1 d2: Explain how histories of religion and empire become universal; Weigh why cultures become successful; Explain how historical success is explained after the fact; Assess the argument that cultures have no clear course
14. **The Discovery of Ignorance** w2 d3: Explain why Europeans took up science and other empires did not; Trace the feedback loop of science, empire and capitalism; Describe the modern scientific worldview and its core ideals; Explain how new powers changed the balance of world dominance
15. **The Marriage of Science and Empire** w2 d3: Describe the role of exploration and mapping in imperial expansion; Explain the role of knowledge in conquest; Explain how science and empire legitimated one another; Weigh the costs and gains of imperialism
16. **The Capitalist Creed** w2 d3: Explain credit and how it expands the economic pie; Explain how trust underwrites credit and growth; Describe the link between capitalism and the state and markets; Weigh capitalism's effects on growth, inequality and empire
17. **The Wheels of Industry** w2 d2: Explain the industrial revolution as converting energy and matter; Describe the role of capitalism in producing demand; Explain how the modern economy shaped the family and daily life; Assess industrial effects on work, consumption and society
18. **A Permanent Revolution** w2 d2: Describe the modern revolution of orders: nuclear, space, biology; Explain how nation and market replaced old orders; Explain how the modern order changes the family and community; Assess what the 'permanent revolution' means for peace and instability
19. **And They Lived Happily Ever After** w2 d3: Explain the role of happiness and its measure in the human story; Compare the meaning view against the biological view of happiness; Assess the claim that history is not a march to happiness; Explain the war on happiness in consumer culture
20. **The End of Homo Sapiens** w2 d3: Describe the emergence of new forms of humanity; Explain the possible paths: biology, machine and genetic engineering; Assess the ethical questions raised about the future of sapiens; Explain what the book means by the end of Homo sapiens
21. **Discovery of Ignorance II** w1 d3: Summarise the book's argument from science to the modern condition; Explain the book's position on progress, meaning and happiness; Assess what the book says about where history is heading; Explain how the book's claims have been criticised and defended
